Lawn pest guide

What is damaging your lawn?

Brown patches, loose turf, small holes or soil mounds do not always mean the same thing. Use the damage pattern and what you find under the turf to narrow down the cause before treating.

Quick lawn pest diagnosis

Choose the symptom that best matches what you see.

Root-feeding larvae are possible. Lift a small edge and inspect the upper soil for chafer grubs or leatherjackets.

Common lawn pests and lawn-disturbing animals

Chafer grubs

What it looks like

White, robust C-shaped larvae with a brown head and three pairs of legs. Some species feed on grass roots. Turf can yellow, loosen, and be torn up by birds or mammals searching for larvae.

How to check

Inspect beneath loose turf. Damage is often most obvious from autumn into spring.

What to do

Biological nematodes such as Heterorhabditis bacteriophora are used against damaging chafer grubs when soil is moist and warm enough; follow supplier instructions and local availability.

Leatherjackets

What it looks like

Grey-brown, tubular, legless larvae of crane flies, often up to about 30 mm long. They can cause yellow-brown dead patches by feeding on roots and stem bases.

How to check

Lift affected turf and inspect the surface soil. Birds may leave small round peck holes while searching for them.

What to do

Nematodes such as Steinernema feltiae or S. carpocapsae are used as biological controls under suitable moist and sufficiently warm soil conditions.

Ants

What it looks like

Ants are important garden organisms and usually do little direct damage to grass. Nests can push fine soil onto the surface and create uneven patches.

How to check

Look for active ants and fine soil heaps directly above nests.

What to do

Where practical, tolerate them. Brush dry soil heaps flat before mowing; intervene only if nests are genuinely disruptive.

Moles

What it looks like

Moles are insect-eating mammals, not root feeders. They create tunnels and molehills that disturb the lawn surface.

How to check

Distinct piles of excavated soil are the key sign.

What to do

Remove or spread molehill soil before mowing and level collapsed surface tunnels. Control choices should follow local wildlife rules.

Birds and other animals

What it looks like

Crows and other animals can tear or peck turf while feeding on chafer grubs or leatherjackets.

How to check

Damage can appear suddenly, but the underlying cause may be larvae in the soil rather than the animal itself.

What to do

Inspect the root zone first. Managing the underlying larval problem can reduce repeated feeding damage.

What not to do

Do not treat every brown patch as an insect problem — drought, disease, fertilizer burn and mowing stress can look similar.
Do not treat every white grub you find. Some scarab larvae live in compost or dead wood and are not lawn pests.
Do not apply insecticides or biological controls without identifying the target and checking the product label, local registration and conditions of use.
Do not reseed repeatedly without correcting the cause of root loss, compaction or poor drainage.

Repair after the cause is controlled

1

Rake out loose dead material

Remove turf that is no longer rooted and lightly loosen the upper soil.

2

Improve the root zone

Correct compaction or drainage problems if they contributed to the damage.

3

Overseed or re-turf

Use a suitable grass mixture and restore good seed-to-soil contact.

4

Keep the repair moist

Maintain moisture through establishment, then transition to deeper, less frequent watering.

Biological control works only under the right conditions. RHS guidance notes that nematodes used for chafer grubs and leatherjackets need moist soil and suitable soil temperature; always follow the supplier’s instructions.
