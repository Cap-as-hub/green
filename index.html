Practical lawn guide

From bare soil to a strong, healthy lawn.

Step by step

How to establish a lawn

The quality of a new lawn is determined mainly by soil preparation, seed-to-soil contact, watering during germination, and correct early mowing.

01

Clear and inspect the area

Remove construction debris, stones, perennial weeds, old turf and roots. Check drainage before adding the final soil layer.

02

Prepare the topsoil

Loosen compacted ground, level the area and create a fine, even seedbed. Avoid leaving deep soft spots that will settle later.

03

Firm and level

Lightly roll or firm the soil. The surface should be stable enough not to sink deeply underfoot, but still loose enough for roots to penetrate.

04

Choose the correct seed mix

Select according to use: decorative, family, sport/high traffic, shade, drought-tolerant or low-maintenance. The species composition matters more than the picture on the bag.

05

Sow evenly

Divide the seed into two equal portions and sow in crossing directions. Lightly rake or press the seed into the surface for good soil contact.

06

Keep the surface moist

During germination, water lightly and frequently enough that the top layer does not dry out. Avoid heavy streams that move the seed.

07

Repair thin areas

After establishment, identify sparse or bare areas. Loosen the surface, add seed, press it in and maintain moisture until the repair germinates.

08

First mowing

Make the first cut when the grass is firmly rooted and has reached roughly 8–10 cm. Cut only the upper third and use a sharp mower blade.

Seed selection

Grass species and lawn mixtures

Most lawn seed products are mixtures of several cool-season grasses. The exact percentage varies by producer, but the species listed below explain how a mixture will behave.

Perennial ryegrass

Lolium perenne

Very fast germination and strong wear tolerance. Excellent for quick establishment, family lawns, repairs and sports use.

Forms underground rhizomes, which help the lawn recover from damage. Dense and durable once established, but slower to germinate.

Nutrition

When and how to fertilize

For cool-season lawns, growth is strongest in spring and autumn. Fertilizing should support that natural rhythm rather than force growth during heat or drought.

Early spring

Goal: restart growth

Use a spring lawn fertilizer with a moderate-to-higher nitrogen share once the lawn is actively growing. Avoid applying heavy nitrogen to frozen or waterlogged soil.

Typical approach

Choose an NPK product where nitrogen (N) is the dominant nutrient. Follow the package rate exactly and water in if required by the label.

Late spring

Goal: density and colour

A balanced lawn fertilizer can support leaf growth and recovery from spring wear. This is often the main growth period.

Typical approach

Moderate nitrogen with potassium. Phosphorus is most useful when soil testing or establishment needs justify it.

Summer

Goal: avoid stress

During hot, dry weather, reduce or postpone nitrogen fertilization. Pushing soft growth during drought can increase stress.

Typical approach

If the lawn is irrigated and actively growing, use a lighter dose. Otherwise wait for cooler, wetter conditions.

Early autumn

Goal: recovery and root strength

Autumn is a key feeding period for cool-season grass, especially after summer stress or overseeding.

Typical approach

Use a lawn fertilizer with useful nitrogen and potassium. Potassium supports stress tolerance and plant strength.

Late autumn

Goal: prepare for winter

Use only products intended for autumn/winter lawn feeding and only while the lawn is still physiologically active.

Typical approach

Lower nitrogen, higher potassium is common in autumn formulations. Avoid excessive late soft growth.

N

Nitrogen

Drives green leaf growth and colour. Too much can produce weak, soft growth, increase mowing demand and raise disease risk.

P

Phosphorus

Important for root development, especially during establishment. Mature lawns often need less unless a soil test shows deficiency.

K

Potassium

Supports stress tolerance, water regulation and winter hardiness. Often emphasized in autumn fertilizers.

Never fertilize by guesswork. Use the product label rate, spread evenly, avoid overlap, and keep granules off paving. If the lawn is severely drought-stressed, wait until it recovers before applying strong nitrogen fertilizer.
Troubleshooting

What to do when grass turns brown

Browning is a symptom, not a diagnosis. First look at the shape and location of the damage: uniform, circular, along edges, after fertilizing, after mowing, or only in compacted areas.

Uniform browning during hot, dry weather

Likely cause: drought stress or temporary summer dormancy.

  • Water deeply rather than giving tiny daily amounts once the lawn is established.
  • Water in the early morning.
  • Raise mowing height during heat.
  • Do not apply strong nitrogen fertilizer to a dry, stressed lawn.
Brown tips immediately after mowing

Likely cause: blunt mower blade, mowing too low or removing too much leaf at once.

  • Sharpen the blade.
  • Follow the one-third rule: remove no more than about one-third of leaf height per cut.
  • Raise mowing height while the lawn recovers.
Brown or yellow stripes after fertilizing

Likely cause: fertilizer burn or overlapping spreader passes.

  • Water the affected area thoroughly if the product label permits.
  • Do not add more fertilizer.
  • Next time, calibrate the spreader and split the application into two crossing passes.
Small dead spots surrounded by darker green grass

Likely cause: concentrated urine, localized salt or chemical damage.

  • Flush the area with water soon after the event.
  • Remove badly damaged turf if necessary and reseed the spot.
Irregular patches that enlarge in humid weather

Possible cause: fungal disease, especially when grass stays wet for long periods.

  • Water early in the day, not late evening.
  • Improve airflow and avoid excessive nitrogen.
  • Remove heavy thatch and reduce prolonged leaf wetness.
Thin grass under trees or beside fences

Likely cause: shade plus competition for water and nutrients.

  • Use a shade-tolerant mixture with fine fescues.
  • Prune where appropriate to improve light and airflow.
  • Accept a slightly higher mowing height.
Bare paths where people repeatedly walk

Likely cause: soil compaction and wear.

  • Aerate compacted soil.
  • Overseed with a wear-tolerant mixture.
  • Reduce traffic until new grass is rooted.
Grass pulls up easily or dies despite watering

Possible cause: shallow rooting, root damage, severe compaction or soil-related problems.

  • Check whether roots are actually developed.
  • Inspect the soil for compaction and drainage problems.
  • Renovate locally if the root zone is poor.
Do not automatically treat every brown patch with fertilizer. Fertilizer can worsen drought stress, salt damage and some disease problems. Diagnose first, then act.
Year-round care

Seasonal Lawn Calendar

A practical cool-season lawn calendar suitable for a northern European climate. Actual timing depends on soil temperature, rainfall and local weather.

Spring

  • Remove winter debris after the soil dries.
  • Rake only when turf is firm enough.
  • Repair winter damage and bare areas.
  • Apply spring fertilizer after active growth starts.
  • Begin mowing with a sharp blade.

Summer

  • Water deeply during dry periods.
  • Raise mowing height in heat.
  • Avoid heavy nitrogen during drought.
  • Watch for disease, dry patches and compaction.
  • Keep mower blades sharp.

Autumn

  • Excellent period for overseeding and repair.
  • Aerate compacted areas if needed.
  • Feed with an autumn-appropriate fertilizer.
  • Remove leaves regularly.
  • Gradually reduce mowing frequency as growth slows.

Winter

  • Avoid traffic on frozen or waterlogged turf.
  • Do not fertilize dormant frozen ground.
  • Service mower and spreader.
  • Plan spring repairs and order seed if needed.
  • Review drainage issues visible after thaw.

Good lawn care is mostly consistency.

Choose a seed mix that suits the actual site, prepare the soil carefully, avoid overwatering and overfertilizing, mow with a sharp blade, and repair weak areas early. A lawn becomes stronger over time when roots are allowed to develop, rather than when growth is forced too aggressively.
